## Introduction

Welcome to the Fudge System Reference Document (SRD). Fudge is a universal role-playing game system designed to be fast, flexible, and easy to learn. It was created by Steffan O'Sullivan and first published in 1995 by Grey Ghost Press, Inc.

This document contains the complete rules of the Fudge system, organized by chapter. Fudge is freely customizable — GMs and players are encouraged to modify any part of the system to suit their campaign needs.

---

## What is Fudge?

Fudge is a role-playing game system that emphasizes character traits described in ordinary language rather than numbers. It uses a seven-level trait scale (Terrible, Poor, Mediocre, Fair, Good, Great, Superb) and simple dice mechanics to resolve actions. Fudge can be adapted to any genre — fantasy, science fiction, historical, modern, or anything else.

The system is built on the philosophy that role-playing should be fun and that rules should serve the story, not the other way around. Fudge is designed to be intuitive and flexible, allowing GMs to add as much or as little detail as they prefer.

---

## Fudge in a Nutshell

In Fudge, characters are defined by traits — Attributes, Skills, Gifts, and Faults — rated on a seven-level scale:

| Level | Adjective |
| ----- | --------- |
| +3    | Superb    |
| +2    | Great     |
| +1    | Good      |
| 0     | Fair      |
| -1    | Mediocre  |
| -2    | Poor      |
| -3    | Terrible  |

An eighth level, Legendary (+4 or more), exists for truly epic abilities.

Action resolution is handled by rolling dice and comparing the result to a character's trait level. Fudge dice (4dF) produce results from -4 to +4, with most results falling between -1 and +1. The result modifies the trait level — a Fair skill (0) with a +1 die roll becomes a Good result.

## Chapter 1 – Character Creation

### 1.1 Character Creation Terms

- **Trait:** Any characteristic that describes a character — Attributes, Skills, Gifts, Faults, and Supernormal Powers are all traits.
- **Level:** A position on the Fudge trait scale (Terrible through Superb).
- **Attribute:** An innate characteristic such as Strength, Dexterity, Intelligence, etc.
- **Skill:** A learned ability, from Acrobatics to Zoology.
- **Gift:** A special advantage — something positive about the character that isn't a skill or attribute.
- **Fault:** A disadvantage or drawback.
- **Supernormal Power:** Any extraordinary ability such as magic, psionics, superpowers, or cybernetics.

### 1.2 Fudge Trait Levels

The standard trait levels are:

| Level    | Value |
| -------- | ----- |
| Superb   | +3    |
| Great    | +2    |
| Good     | +1    |
| Fair     | 0     |
| Mediocre | -1    |
| Poor     | -2    |
| Terrible | -3    |

**Legendary** (+4 or higher) may be used for truly epic abilities.

### 1.3 Character Traits

#### 1.3.1 Attributes

Attributes are innate capabilities that every character possesses to some degree. The GM decides which attributes to use. Common examples include:

- Strength, Dexterity, Constitution/Health, Intelligence, Wisdom, Perception, Willpower, Charisma, Luck

Attributes default to Fair unless the player chooses to raise or lower them.

#### 1.3.2 Skills

Skills are learned abilities. They default to Poor (or Non-Existent in some cases) unless the character has been trained. The GM determines which skills are available in the campaign. Skills may be broad (e.g., Combat) or narrow (e.g., Sword).

#### 1.3.3 Gifts

Gifts are positive traits that give the character an edge. Examples include:

- Combat Reflexes, Night Vision, Wealthy, Attractive, Lucky, Animal Empathy

#### 1.3.4 Faults

Faults are disadvantages that hinder the character. Examples include:

- Cowardice, Greedy, Curious, Code of Honor, Enemy, Poor

#### 1.3.5 Personality

Characters may have personality traits that aren't mechanically defined but guide role-playing. The GM may award bonuses or penalties based on how well a player portrays their character's personality.

#### 1.3.6 Fudge Points

Fudge Points allow players to influence the game in their favor. They can be used to:

- Reroll a bad result
- Add +1 to a die roll
- Reduce damage by one level
- Declare a coincidental event

The GM awards Fudge Points for good role-playing, clever ideas, and other contributions to the game.

### 1.4 Allocating Traits

Characters are created by allocating levels to traits. The GM sets limits on how many levels can be spent, what maximum levels are allowed, etc. There are two main approaches: Subjective and Objective Character Creation.

### 1.5 Subjective Character Creation

In Subjective Character Creation, players simply describe their characters and assign trait levels that feel appropriate. The GM reviews and approves the character. There are no point costs or formulas — the GM uses judgment to ensure balance.

### 1.6 Objective Character Creation

Objective Character Creation uses point costs to ensure balance. Each trait level costs a certain number of "levels" or "points."

#### 1.6.1 Attributes

The default attribute level is Fair (0). Raising an attribute costs one level per step. Lowering an attribute refunds one level per step.

**Typical attribute costs:** Superb (+3), Great (+2), Good (+1), Fair (0), Mediocre (-1), Poor (-2), Terrible (-3)

#### 1.6.2 Skills

Skills default to Poor unless specified otherwise. Raising a skill costs levels as follows:

| Level    | Easy    | Normal  | Hard    | Very Hard |
| -------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | --------- |
| Superb   | 4       | 5       | 6       | 7         |
| Great    | 3       | 4       | 5       | 6         |
| Good     | 2       | 3       | 4       | 5         |
| Fair     | 1       | 2       | 3       | 4         |
| Mediocre | 0       | 1       | 2       | 3         |
| Poor     | default | default | default | default   |
| Terrible | -1      | -1      | -1      | -1        |

#### 1.6.3 Gifts & Faults

Gifts and Faults are balanced against each other. Typically each Gift costs 1 "slot" and each Fault grants 1 "slot." Characters start with a certain number of free Gift slots.

#### 1.6.4 Trading Traits

Traits may be traded off against each other:

- 3 skill levels = 1 attribute level
- 6 skill levels = 1 Gift
- 1 Fault = 1 Gift or 1 attribute level

### 1.7 Uncommitted Traits

Uncommitted traits are skills or attributes not yet defined during character creation. The player leaves them blank, then defines them during play as needed.

### 1.8 Random Character Creation

The GM may optionally use dice to randomly determine trait levels.

### 1.9 Minimizing Abuse

The GM should review all characters for balance, require justification for high trait levels, and ensure gifts and faults are appropriate to the campaign.

## Chapter 2 – Supernormal Powers

### 2.1 Terms

- **Supernormal Power:** Any extraordinary ability beyond normal human capability.
- **Scale:** A measure of size, strength, or technological superiority.
- **Power Level:** The effectiveness level of a supernormal power.

### 2.2 Powers at Character Creation

#### 2.2.1 Powers Available

The GM decides which supernormal powers exist in the campaign: Magic, Miracles, Psi, Superpowers, Cybernetic Enhancements, Racial Abilities.

#### 2.2.2 Associated Skills

Using a supernormal power usually requires a related skill (e.g., Spell-casting for magic).

#### 2.2.3 Combat Powers

Powers that affect combat use the combat resolution system, often with the power adding to damage or providing special effects.

### 2.3 Non-humans

#### 2.3.1 Strength and Mass

Non-human characters may have different Strength and Mass Scales. Scale +1 = roughly double human capability; -1 = roughly half.

#### 2.3.2 Speed

Speed can be expressed as Scale for creatures significantly faster or slower than humans.

#### 2.3.3 Scale Correlations

| Scale | Example          |
| ----- | ---------------- |
| +10   | Giant, Kaiju     |
| +8    | Dragon, Elephant |
| +6    | Large giant      |
| +4    | Ogre, Horse      |
| +2    | Large human      |
| 0     | Human            |
| -2    | Halfling         |
| -4    | Small child      |
| -6    | Cat, large bird  |
| -8    | Rat, small bird  |
| -10   | Pixie, mouse     |

#### 2.3.4 Cost of Scale

Scale is priced as a Supernormal Power.

#### 2.3.5 Racial Bonuses

Racial bonuses are treated as Gifts or Supernormal Powers.

### 2.4 Legendary Heroes

May have trait levels above Superb, known as Legendary (+4) or higher.

### 2.5 Magic

The GM decides: who can cast spells, how magic works, the source of power, and the cost of using magic.

### 2.6 Miracles

Miracles are granted by deities. Characters petition through prayer or ritual.

### 2.7 Psi

Psionic powers require: a Power (innate capability), a Skill (control), and Psychic Reservoir (energy reserve).

### 2.8 Superpowers

Any extraordinary abilities common in superhero genres: flight, super-strength, energy blasts, invulnerability, etc.

### 2.9 Cybernetic Enhancements

Replace or augment body parts with technological equivalents. Treated as Gifts or Supernormal Powers.

## Chapter 3 – Action Resolution

### 3.1 Terms

- **Action:** Any attempt by a character to do something.
- **Difficulty Level (DL):** The minimum result needed to succeed.
- **Relative Degree:** The difference between two opposed results.
- **Opposed Action:** Two characters contest each other.
- **Unopposed Action:** Only one character's effort matters.

### 3.2 Rolling the Dice

#### 3.2.1 Fudge Dice

Fudge uses four six-sided dice marked with two +, two -, and two blank sides. Roll 4dF and total:

- Each + = +1
- Each - = -1
- Each blank = 0

Results range from -4 to +4.

#### 3.2.2 Other Dice Techniques

- **4d6 method:** Roll 4d6, count 1-2 as -, 3-4 as 0, 5-6 as +.
- **1d6 method:** Roll 1d6: 1=-3, 2=-2, 3=-1, 4=+1, 5=+2, 6=+3.
- **Percentile method:** Use a d% table (see Fantasy Fudge chapter).

#### 3.2.3 Success Rates

| Target       | 4dF chance | 1d6 chance |
| ------------ | ---------- | ---------- |
| +3 or better | 1%         | 17%        |
| +2 or better | 6%         | 33%        |
| +1 or better | 18%        | 50%        |
| 0 or better  | 38%        | 67%        |
| -1 or better | 62%        | 83%        |
| -2 or better | 82%        | 100%       |
| -3 or better | 94%        | 100%       |

### 3.3 Action Modifiers

The GM may apply modifiers from -4 to +4 based on circumstances:

- Advantageous: +1 to +2
- Disadvantageous: -1 to -2
- Extremely favorable/unfavorable: +3/+4 or -3/-4

### 3.4 Unopposed Actions

GM sets a Difficulty Level (DL). Player rolls and adds result to trait level. If final result equals or exceeds DL, the action succeeds.

| Difficulty     | Level    |
| -------------- | -------- |
| Trivially easy | Terrible |
| Very easy      | Poor     |
| Easy           | Mediocre |
| Average        | Fair     |
| Hard           | Good     |
| Very hard      | Great    |
| Extremely hard | Superb   |

### 3.5 Opposed Actions

Both participants roll. Compare modified results:

- If A > B: A wins by the difference (relative degree)
- If equal: Tie — status quo maintained

### 3.6 Critical Results

A roll of +4 is a critical success (if successful); a roll of -4 is a critical failure (if unsuccessful).

### 3.7 NPC Reactions

Situational Roll to determine NPC attitudes:

- +4: Extremely friendly/helpful
- +3: Very friendly
- +2: Friendly
- +1: Receptive
- 0: Neutral/uncertain
- -1: Unreceptive
- -2: Hostile
- -3: Very hostile
- -4: Violently hostile

## Chapter 4 – Combat

Unless one participant is unaware of an attack or decides to ignore it, combat is an Opposed action in Fudge. The easiest way to handle combat in Fudge is as a series of Opposed actions. This can be done simply or with more complexity. The author of Fudge uses simple and loose combat rules in order to get combat over with quickly and get back to more interesting role-playing. This chapter, largely optional, is for players who prefer combat options spelled out in detail.

Melee combat and Ranged combat are treated separately.

### 4.1 – Combat Terms

**Melee:** any combat that involves striking the opponent with a fist or hand-held weapon. Any attack from further away is a Ranged attack.

**Story Element:** a distinct segment of the storyline in the game. In combat, the interval between story elements can be a practical place for a die roll.

**Combat Round:** an indeterminate length of time set by the GM — around three seconds seems reasonable to some people, while that seems grossly short or absurdly long to others. A given GM's combat round may vary in length, depending on the situation. Generally, when each character involved has made an action, a given round is over.

**Offensive damage factors:** those which contribute to damaging an opponent: Strength (if using a Strength-driven weapon), Scale, and deadliness of weapon.

**Defensive damage factors:** those which contribute to reducing the severity of a received blow: Scale, armor, and possibly Damage Capacity.

**Total damage factor** (or simply damage factor): the attacker's offensive damage factor minus the defender's defensive damage factor.

### 4.2 – Melee Combat

Fudge gives three options available for handling the pacing of melee combat: moving from story element to story element, using simultaneous combat rounds, or alternating combat turns. An individual GM may devise others.

#### 4.2.1 – Story Elements

In the simplest combat system, the GM explains the situation in as much detail as is apparent, then asks the players to describe what their characters are doing. The more complete the description of their characters' actions, the better the GM knows how to assess the situation. This can be important if she has something that won't be revealed until the middle of a battle. Die rolls, if any, are required by the GM for each story element.

A story element is the smallest unit of time in this type of combat resolution. The GM may break the battle down into several story elements, or treat the whole encounter as one element. This depends on the GM's style, the importance of the battle, the number of participants, whether or not there are unexpected surprises, etc. Each element should be a dramatic unit.

For example, the PCs are faced with a detachment of guards at the door while the evil mastermind is trying to activate the Doomsday machine at the back of the room. The fight with the guards might be one element while the confrontation with Dr. Doomsday could be a second. Another GM might treat the whole battle as one story element, while a third GM would treat each 5-second segment separately. Whatever the number of elements, keep the battle description as word-oriented as possible.

The GM may ask for a single die roll from a player occasionally, or require three rolls and take the median roll.

(The median is the middle value die roll, which may be the same as either the high or low die roll. For example, if the player rolls a Good, a Mediocre, and a Superb result, the median is Good, since it's the result in between Mediocre and Superb. But a result of Poor, Great, and Great gives a median die roll of Great. Using a median tends to soften the role of extreme luck. Some GMs use a median when a single die result represents many actions.)

Once the GM has decided which trait (or traits) each PC should use for this combat, she then gives them a modifier, ranging from -3 to +3. Zero should be the most common modifier. The modifier is based partly on how well the PCs' plan would work, given what the GM knows of the NPCs, and partly on circumstances: fatigue, lighting, footing, surprise, weapon superiority, bravery or cowardice of NPCs, wounds, etc.

**Example:** Gunner, separated from the other PCs, surprises five members of a rival gang in a garage. The player announces that Gunner will shout and charge the rival mob, carrying his Tommy gun as if he's about to fire — they don't know it's irreparably jammed. He hopes to see them run away, hit the dirt, or freeze in fear. He'll then use his Tommy gun as a club, starting at the left end of their line. He'll keep his current opponent in between him and the others as long as possible. He hopes to then roll up their line, one at a time, keeping the wall to his left side as he charges.

The GM makes a Situational roll for the mob: Mediocre. The mob members don't recover quickly from their surprise, so she gives Gunner a +1 to his Brawling skill of Good for this plan. She also decides that one mobster will run away and the others won't draw their guns until Gunner has already engaged the first enemy. His Running skill is Great, so she gives him another +1, since he can cover ground quickly. Total modifier for Gunner is +2, bringing his Brawling skill to Superb for this combat. Since this is a fairly long action and she doesn't want a single unlucky roll to ruin Gunner's chances, she asks him for three Brawling skill rolls (at the +2 modifier), and to use the median roll.

Gunner rolls a Good, Superb, and Great result, in that order. The median roll is Great, and the GM decides this is good enough to have downed the first two mobsters, and describes the battle so far in entertaining detail. Now Gunner is facing the last two thugs, who finally have their pistols out and could probably plug him before he charges that far. The GM asks, "What does Gunner do now?"

Gunner hurls the Tommy gun into the face of one gunman while making a low diving tackle for the other, hoping to dodge under any bullets. The GM calls for a single roll against Brawling to cover this whole action: Gunner gets a Fair result. The GM rules that Gunner throws the Tommy gun well enough to distract one gunman, but not harm him. He does, however, manage to tackle and subdue his other foe, whose shots all go wild.

At this point, the GM rules that the mobster grazed by the thrown Tommy gun now steps over and points his pistol to Gunner's head while he's kneeling over the other mobster. Gunner wisely heeds the call to surrender and hopes his friends can rescue him.

#### 4.2.2 – Simultaneous Combat Rounds

Those who like their combat broken down into discrete bits can use combat "rounds." In simultaneous action rounds, all offensive and defensive maneuvers happen at the same time. This is realistic: few real combats consist of fighters taking turns whacking at each other.

The GM determines which traits the combatants should roll against. This depends largely on which weapon they are using, which might simply be a fist. Weapon type also affects damage — see Section 4.5, Wounds.

Each combatant makes an Opposed action roll. On a relative degree of zero, the combat round is a stand-off — the fighters either circled each other looking for an opening, or exchanged blows on each other's shields, etc. — nobody is hurt.

A minimum result of Poor is needed to hit a (roughly) equal-sized opponent. That is, a human needs to score a Poor blow (and still win the Opposed action) in order to hit another human. If both opponents roll worse than Poor, the round is a standoff.

If one opponent is significantly bigger than the other (of a different Scale, at least), he needs a Mediocre or even Fair result to hit his smaller foe, while even a Terrible result will allow the small fighter to hit the larger. (Of course, such a blow must still win the Opposed action.) Extremely small targets, such as a pixie, may require a Good or even a Great result. Examples include humans fighting giants, or very large or small animals.

If the result is a relative degree other than zero, and the minimum level needed to score a hit is achieved or surpassed, the winner checks to see if he hit hard enough to damage the loser. In general, the better the hit (the greater the relative degree), the greater the likelihood of damage.

If one combatant is unable to fight in a given round (possibly because he's unaware of the attacker, or because of a critical result in the previous round — see Section 3.6, Critical Results), the combat may become an Unopposed Action for the active fighter, usually with a Poor Difficulty Level. If a character can defend himself in some way, such as using a shield, it is still an Opposed Action, but the defending character cannot hurt the other character even if he wins the combat round.

Combat often takes more than one combat round. Characters are not limited to attacking each round — they may attempt to flee, negotiate, try a fancy acrobatic stunt, or any other appropriate action.

#### 4.2.3 – Alternating Combat Turns

Using alternating combat turns, each combat round consists of two actions: the fighter with the higher initiative attacks while the other defends, then the second combatant attacks while the first defends. With multiple characters involved in combat, the side with the initiative makes all their attacks, then the other side makes all their attacks. Or the GM may run the combat in initiative order, even if fighters from both sides are interspersed throughout the combat turn.

Gaining initiative is an Opposed action. If the characters don't have an Initiative attribute or skill — such as Reflexes or Speed — simply use Opposed Situational rolls. A gift such as Combat Reflexes can grant a +1 to initiative. Surprise may grant a bonus to the roll, or give automatic initiative. Initiative can be rolled once for each battle or once each round. Perhaps a character could trade skill for initiative: attack hastily (+1 to initiative that round) but be slightly off balance because of it (-1 to attack and defend that round).

Each attack is an Opposed Action: the attacker's Offensive skill (Sword, Melee Weapon, Martial Art, etc.) against a defender's Defensive skill (Shield, Parry, Dodge, Duck, etc.). This type of combat take longer than simultaneous rounds, but some players feel it gives a character more control over his own fate.

Using these rules, a Defensive parry skill may simply equal the weapon skill, or it may be a separate skill that must be bought independently of an Offensive skill. The GM must tell the players at character creation which method she is using — or allow them extra levels on the fly to adjust their defensive abilities.

Some weapons, such as an Axe, are poor parrying weapons. Players should ask the GM at character creation if a weapon may be used to parry and still be used to attack without penalty in the next turn — and give their characters decent Shield or Dodge skills to compensate for poor parrying weapons.

All-out offensive and defensive tactics can be used. A character forfeits his attack for a round if he chooses All-out defense, and is at -2 on his defense on his opponent's next turn if choosing All-out offense — or perhaps gets no defense at all!

### 4.3 – Melee Combat Options

#### 4.3.1 – Melee Modifiers

- A fighter who is Hurt is at -1, while one who is Very Hurt is at -2.
- Positional advantage: -1 or -2 penalty to the fighter in the worse position.
- Shield value subtracts from opponent's weapon skill. Small shield: +1 melee. Medium shield: +1 melee, +1 vs. ranged. Large shield: +2 all combat.
- Weapon + shield comparison: if one fighter's value is +2+ greater, smaller weapon user is at -1.
- Called shots: minimum result of Good or Great to hit, with -1 to trait level.
- Magical blessings (+1) or curses (-1).
- All-out offense: +1 combat skill, +1 damage if successful; if tie/loss, opponent gets +2 damage.
- All-out defense: +2 combat skill, cannot harm foe except with critical result.
- Successful all-out defense + Perception/Tactics roll: -1 penalty to opponent next round.

#### 4.3.2 – Offensive/Defensive Tactics

Before each round, choose a posture:

1. +2 Offense, -2 Defense
2. +1 Offense, -1 Defense
3. Normal
4. -1 Offense, +1 Defense
5. -2 Offense, +2 Defense

Both sides secretly choose and reveal simultaneously. Roll once; results applied to both offense and defense separately.

#### 4.3.3 – PCs vs. NPCs

Treat as Unopposed action; NPC always gets trait level result. PC must tie for standoff, beat for damage.

#### 4.3.4 – Multiple Combatants

Lone fighter at -1 per extra foe beyond first. Roll once; compare to each opponent's result. Must defeat/tie all to wound one. If tie best opponent, can wound another whose result is at least two levels below. Max 6 attackers simultaneously under ideal conditions.

#### 4.3.5 – Hit Location

Simplest: better relative degree = better location. Called shots: minimum relative degree 2-4 to hit specific location. If hit but not by enough, defender names location hit.

Damaged body parts: Scratched (no effect), Hurt (-1 to use), Incapacitated.

#### 4.3.6 – Fancy Stuff

Speed/Reflexes: allow extra attacks. "Plus" levels for finer combat skill granularity: e.g., Good+ gets +1 every other round. Swashbuckling: reward good descriptions with bonuses.

### 4.4 – Ranged Combat

Target unaware: Unopposed action, GM sets DL. Target aware: Opposed action (attacker's skill vs. defender's Dodge). Propelled weapons: defender at -2 or -3. Declining to dodge to shoot back: Unopposed for both, may be simultaneous.

Guns: damage ratings set by GM. Small handgun: +2 to +3 Strength. Derringer: +0 to +1. Powerful two-handed: +5+. Bazookas: +10+. Automatic weapons: more bullets hit with higher relative degrees.

### 4.5 – Wounds

#### 4.5.1 – Wound Levels

- **Undamaged:** No combat wounds.
- **Just A Scratch:** No game effect.
- **Hurt:** -1 to all affected traits.
- **Very Hurt:** -2 to all affected traits.
- **Incapacitated:** Cannot act.
- **Near Death:** Unconscious, will die within an hour without help.
- **Dead:** No more use for possessions.

#### 4.5.2 – Damage Capacity

Option 1: Attribute rated +3 to -3, added to defense. Option 2: Die roll on hit (Great+ reduces wound, Poor+ increases). Option 3: Gift/Fault (Damage Resistant/Fragile). Option 4: Damage Capacity reduced by hits. Option 5: Willpower roll instead.

#### 4.5.3 – Wound Factors

1. Relative degree of attack success.
2. Strength of blow (muscle-powered) or weapon power.
3. Weapon deadliness.
4. Defender's armor.
5. Victim's Damage Capacity/Mass.

#### 4.5.4 – Sample Wound Factors

**Offensive:**

- Strength: +3 Superb to -3 Terrible
- Attacker's Scale: add Strength Scale
- Weapon Strength (guns, etc.): +/- weapon rating
- Muscle-powered weapon: -1 (no weapon), +0 (small), +1 (medium), +2 (large 1-handed or light 2-handed), +3 (heavy 2-handed), +1 sharpness

**Defensive:**

- Damage Capacity: +3 Superb to -3 Terrible
- Armor: +1 light non-metal, +2 heavy non-metal or light metal, +3 medium metal, +4 heavy metal, +5+ SF armor
- Defender's Mass Scale

#### 4.5.5 – Determining Wound Level

Simple: GM assesses factors and announces result. Numerical: total damage factor = offensive factors - defensive factors + relative degree. Compare to wound table:

| Damage | 1-2     | 3-4  | 5-6       | 7-8    | 9+         |
| ------ | ------- | ---- | --------- | ------ | ---------- |
| Wound  | Scratch | Hurt | Very Hurt | Incap. | Near Death |

#### 4.5.6 – Grazing

Relative degree +1 = graze at most. Sample graze severity: <0 = undamaged, 0-4 = Scratch, 5+ = Hurt.

#### 4.5.7 – Recording Wounds

Use boxes on character sheet. Check off appropriate box for each wound received. If no box open for that level, use next highest. Only highest wound penalty counts. Three scratch boxes default (more for cinematic campaigns).

#### 4.5.8 – Non-human Scale in Combat

Add attacker's Strength Scale to offense, defender's Mass Scale to defense. Scale piercing weapons halve or ignore Scale.

**Examples:** Sheba (human) vs. McMurtree (leprechaun Scale -4): damage factor +4. Fionn (Scale -4) vs. Sheba: damage factor -3. Wilbur vs. Dragon (Scale +6): damage factor -2, Scratch on +3 hit.

### 4.6 – Wound Options

#### 4.6.1 – Damage Die Roll

Roll 1dF (-1,0,+1) added to damage factor or wound level. Alternatively, use combat die roll parity (even = +1 to offense, odd = unchanged). Situational roll from -4 to +4 added to damage number.

#### 4.6.2 – Stun, Knockout, Pulling Punches

Stun: Hurt → Stun (no attack, -1 defend, 1 round). Very Stunned: -2 for 2 rounds. Incapacitated → knockout. Pulling punches: announce max wound level.

#### 4.6.3 – Min-Mid-Max Die Roll

Roll 3d6 for damage. Read Min, Mid, or Max based on damage factor and relative degree bonuses. Total bonus <0: no damage. 0: Min. 1: Mid. 2: Max. 3: Max+Min. 4: All three.

#### 4.6.4 – PC Death

Options: (1) Max 3 wound levels in one blow. (2) Cannot reach Near Death unless already Incapacitated. (3) Spend Fudge Point to reduce deadly wound.

#### 4.6.5 – Technological Levels as Scale

Tech differences expressed as Scale between weapons and armor of different eras.

Full example of Snorri vs. Brynhild with round-by-round narration demonstrating damage factors, all-out attacks/defense, grazes, and situational advantages.

### 4.8 – Healing

Scratches heal after 5-10 minutes. Good healing roll: heal one wound level. Great: two levels. Superb: three levels. Natural healing: one level per week rest.

### 7.1 – Sample Magic System: Fudge Magic

Date: November, 1992 to November, 1993 By: Steffan O'Sullivan

Here is a sample magic system, based on the following premises mentioned in Section 2.5, Magic:

- Who can cast: Magicians only (supernormal power needed).
- Levels of Power: yes. The greater the power, the easier to cast powerful spells; power levels act as a reserve in case of severe failure.
- Source of Power: manipulation of local area mana.
- Reliability: Fair.
- Time to cast: depends on potency of spell (one minute to days).
- Spells: improvised.
- Material Components: none needed, but good use can give +1.
- Drawbacks: casting non-trivial spells is fatiguing; severe failure causes distress.

#### 7.1.1 – Magic Potential

Magic Potential is a Supernormal Power (cost: 2 gifts per level). A character with at least one level is a "magician." Each level must be specialized (e.g., Combat Magic, Healing Magic, Illusion). Failing a spell miserably causes temporary loss of one level of Magic Potential; at -1 the magician falls into a coma. Recharged by resting one week per level.

#### 7.1.2 – Spells

Player describes desired result; GM assesses potency (Trivial, Average, Potent). Potency determines Difficulty Level (Fair for average). Duration set by GM. Fatigue costs apply.

#### 7.1.3 – Mana

Mana is an energy source. Local mana density affects casting difficulty. Same effect in same area within 24 hours: cumulative -1 per casting. Mana-rich: +1/+2. Mana-poor: -1/-2.

#### 7.1.4 – Skill

Spell-casting skill default: Non-Existent. Maximum base level: Fair. Modified by time taken, effort, mana density, multiple casters, etc.

#### 7.1.5 – Resolution

Unopposed action vs. spell potency DL. Surpass: spell works. Equal: watered-down version. Fail: stunned 1 round, fatigue. Roll of -4: always fails, drains one level of Magic Potential.

#### 7.1.6 – Personal Magic Resistance

Control spells: Opposed action vs. subject's Magic Resistance (typically Willpower).

#### 7.1.7 – Certain Spell-Casting

Drain one level of Magic Potential for guaranteed success (but magician faints after casting).

#### 7.1.8 – Enchanting Items

Works for weeks/months. Roll against Spell-casting and appropriate Craft skill.

#### 7.1.9 – Fudge Magic Options

Generalized Magic Potential, non-magicians casting, alternative skill caps, less risky casting.

### 7.2 – Sample Miracle System: Fudge Miracles

Date: December, 1992, By: Steffan O'Sullivan

Miracles granted by petition. Divine Favor (2 supernormal powers per step). Unopposed action vs. Petitioning skill. Good+ = granted. Fair/Mediocre = no answer. Poor- = deity angered.

Modifiers: behavior (+/-1), furthering deity's desires (+1), sphere of influence (+1), trivial request (-1), etc.

### 7.3 – Sample Psionic System: Fudge Psi

Date: February, 1993 & December, 1993, By: Shawn Garbett and Steffan O'Sullivan

Three types: Powers, skills, Psychic Reservoir.

#### 7.3.1 – Psionic Powers

Each Power costs 1 Supernormal Power (2 gifts). Power levels define range, quantity, size.

**Very Broad Groups:** Antipsi, ESP, Psychokinesis, Telepathy, Vampirism, Teleportation, Planar Travel.

#### 7.3.2 – Psionic Skills

Each Power requires a control skill. Default: Non-Existent. Max starting level: Fair.

#### 7.3.3 – Psychic Reservoir

Default: Fair for all characters. Drained by psi use. Below Terrible: unconscious. Regained by rest (1 week per level).

#### 7.3.4 – Psionic Actions

Opposed (vs. Willpower) or Unopposed. Augment result by sacrificing Psychic Reservoir levels. Terrible result: backfire + lose Reservoir level.

#### 7.3.5 – Desperation Psionics

Drain Reservoir before roll to increase Power level (+1 per level), but at -2 per level to skill.

#### 7.3.6 – Psi Modifiers

Mediocre PR: -1, Poor: -2, Terrible: -3. Skill augmentation: +1 per level drained. Desperation: -2 per level. Power 3+ above minimum: +1.

#### 7.3.7 – Psi Examples

Yardmower Man uses Telekinesis to mow lawn, then attempts Molecular Rearrangement on psi director. Full example with augmentation rolls.

### 7.4 – Alternate Rules

#### 7.4.1 – Alternate Character Creation (Ed Heil)

Create characters as game progresses. GM assigns skill levels available during session. Traits defined when first used.

#### 7.4.2 – Diceless Action Resolution (Reimer Behrends)

GM decides outcomes based on cause and effect, detailed description, genre conventions. Fudge Points let players decide outcomes.

**Diceless Combat:** Use story elements or rounds. "Post warning signs" before danger. Describe actions fully. Wounds described in narrative detail.

#### 7.4.3 – Rolling the Dice (Andy Skinner)

Players who roll +4 may roll again; if positive, add to +4. Allows results up to +8.

#### 7.4.4 – Heroic Evasion (Peter Bonney & Steffan O'Sullivan)

Hit character may reduce wound by one level by throwing themselves out of the way. At -2 next combat round, cumulative -1 per successive use.

#### 7.4.5 – Recording Wounds with Cards (Bernard Hsiung)

Use playing cards: face-down = Hurt, face-up = fatigue. Each card = -1. Black = torso, red = extremity.

## Chapter 8 – Fantasy Fudge

A Sample Implementation of Fudge by Ann Dupuis, Based on "Five Point Fudge" by Steffan O'Sullivan

Five-point Fudge is an alternative character creation system. This version is for a Fantasy genre only.

### 8.1 – Character Creation

Standard seven-level trait scale. Steps: 1) Decide character concept. 2) Spend points on skills from 2+ Skill Groups. 3) Assign Attributes. 4) Choose Gifts (2). 5) Choose Faults (2). 6) Adjust with trading.

#### 8.1.1 – Character Points

5 points to purchase skills. Max 4 points in any group. Must spend in at least 2 groups.

**Point expenditure table:**

| Points | Broad Focus                                   | Narrow Focus                     |
| ------ | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1      | 3 at Fair, 1 at Mediocre                      | 1 at Good, 1 at Mediocre         |
| 2      | 2 at Good, 4 at Fair                          | 1 at Great, 1 at Good, 1 at Fair |
| 3      | 1 at Great, 3 at Good, 4 at Fair              | —                                |
| 4      | 1 at Superb, 2 at Great, 3 at Good, 3 at Fair | —                                |

**General Skills Point:** Max 1 point. Any 3 skills at Fair from 2-3 groups.

**Trading:** 1 skill for 2 at one level lower. Attribute levels tradeable. 1 extra Fault = 1 Gift or 1 Attribute level.

**Possible point combinations:** 5 groups (1,1,1,1,1), 4 groups (2,1,1,1), 3 groups (3,1,1 or 2,2,1), 2 groups (4,1 or 3,2).

Four types: Innate, Hedge, Scholarly, Clerical.

Gift: Magical Talent: Innate. Each Gift provides one type: Dowsing, Eagle Eyes, Fire-Starter, Fortune Telling, Green Thumb, Healing Hands, Second Sight, Shapeshifter (2 Gifts). No skill roll required; automatic at Great level.

**Sample Spells:** "Skinwalker" (Transmutation, Good DL, 2 PP, 20 min). "Targeted Fireball" (Conjuration+Elementalism+Kineticism, Fair DL, 1 PP, 1 round).

### 8.5 – Equipping Characters

Starting equipment package. Customization allowed. Determine Offensive and Defensive Damage Factors for weapons and armor.

### 8.6 – Character Development

Award 1-3 Fudge Points per session. 3 FP = 1 EP. EP spent to raise traits (see Chapter 5).

### 8.7 – Settings

Use with any fantasy world. Grey Ghost website has links to Fudge game settings.

### 8.8 – Action Resolution

Two types: Unopposed (DL set by GM) and Opposed (both roll, compare). Very easy = automatic. Very hard = impossible. Default DL: Fair.

#### 8.8.1 – Rolling the Dice

**Percentile conversion table:**

| Roll       | 1         | 2-6       | 7-18      | 19-38  | 39-62 |
| ---------- | --------- | --------- | --------- | ------ | ----- |
| Result     | -4        | -3        | -2        | -1     | +0    |
| **Roll**   | **63-82** | **83-94** | **95-99** | **00** |       |
| **Result** | +1        | +2        | +3        | +4     |       |

#### 8.8.2 – Unopposed Actions

GM sets DL. Roll + skill vs. DL. Success if equal or exceed.

#### 8.8.3 – Opposed Actions

Both roll. Winner = higher result. Relative Degree = difference. In combat, add Relative Degree to Offensive Damage Factor.

### 8.9 – Combat

1 round ≈ 3 seconds. Simultaneous actions. Opposed for melee; Unopposed for ranged/unaware/cannon-fodder.

#### 8.9.1 – Special Circumstances

Terrible hit never damages. Multiple opponents: lone fighter at -1 per extra foe. Cannon fodder: always get trait level result.

#### 8.9.2 – Wounds

Formula: ODF + Relative Degree - DDF = Damage Points. Compare to wound table:

| Damage | 1-2     | 3-4  | 5-6       | 7-8    | 9+         |
| ------ | ------- | ---- | --------- | ------ | ---------- |
| Wound  | Scratch | Hurt | Very Hurt | Incap. | Near Death |

Penalties: Scratch = 0, Hurt = -1, Very Hurt = -2, Incapacitated = can't act, Near Death = dying.

#### 8.9.3 – Stun, Knockout, Pulling Punches

Stun = Hurt result (1 round). Very Stunned = Very Hurt (2 rounds). Incapacitated = knockout.

### 8.10 – Situational Rolls

For luck, outside events. Use for NPC morale: -1 or worse = surrender/flee.

### 8.11 – Fudge Points

Uses: auto-success on unopposed action, alter die roll 1 level, reduce wound 1 level, get automatic +4 result.

### 8.12 – The Only Rule You Really Need To Know

When in doubt, just fudge it! Have things happen in a way that lets players have fun.

**Troll:** Scale +3. Toughness (+3 DDF), Stench (-2 opponent combat). ODF +5 claws, +8 club.

**Wall Crawler:** Scale -6. Toxic Spines (Fair Health or hallucinate 2-4 hours). 7-12 in group.

**Zombie:** Immune to Pain and Fear. Tough (+2 DDF). Mindless. Need Near Death to "kill."

**Zuvembie:** Retains some intelligence. Immune to Pain. Skills -2 from life. Tough (+2 DDF). Need Near Death to "kill."

### Damage Factor Summary

**Offensive Damage Factors:**

- Strength: +3 (Superb) to -3 (Terrible)
- Muscle Weapon: -1 (none) to +3 (two-handed) + sharpness
- Scale: Attacker's Strength Scale

**Defensive Damage Factors:**

- Damage Capacity: +3 (Superb) to -3 (Terrible)
- Armor: +1 (light) to +5+ (SF)
- Scale: Defender's Mass Scale
