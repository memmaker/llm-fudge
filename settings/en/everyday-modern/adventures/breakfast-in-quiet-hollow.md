The morning starts the same way it always does in Quiet Hollow: the hum of the refrigerator, the soft clink of a spoon against a chipped mug, and the low murmur of a local radio station reciting yesterday’s news as if it might somehow matter more today. Your kitchen window looks out over a street that hasn’t changed in years—same parked cars, same neighbors leaving for work at the same times, same sense that nothing much ever happens here. You’re halfway through your breakfast when your phone buzzes with a message that doesn’t fit the routine.

It’s from your supervisor. Not a reminder, not a schedule change—just a short, tense request: "We need to talk. Today." Before you can process that, another message comes in, this time from a neighbor you barely know, asking if you saw anything unusual last night. You didn’t—or at least, you don’t think you did. But as you sit there, replaying the evening in your mind, small details begin to surface: a car idling too long, voices drifting through the open window, something that felt off but easy to ignore at the time.

By the time you finish your coffee, the ordinary weight of the day has shifted. Work suddenly feels uncertain, your quiet street feels watchful, and whatever happened last night may be closer to you than you’d like. In a town where nothing is supposed to happen, even a small disruption has a way of pulling at loose threads—of routines, relationships, and the stories people tell themselves to keep things simple.

Stepping outside means deciding what matters more: keeping your head down and protecting your own stability, or getting involved in something that could ripple through your job, your neighbors, and your sense of how safe and predictable your life really is.
